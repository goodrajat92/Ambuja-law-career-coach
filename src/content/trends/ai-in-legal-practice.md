Every year brings a new wave of "AI will transform the legal profession" commentary. The more useful question for a working deal lawyer is narrower: **which parts of the job has it actually started changing?**

## Where it's genuinely useful today

- **First-pass document review.** AI tools are reasonably good at flagging *where* to look in a large due-diligence document set — unusual clauses, missing signature pages, inconsistent defined terms — even when they're not reliable on the substantive legal conclusion. Treat the output as a triage list, not a memo.
- **First drafts of routine documents.** NDAs, standard board resolutions, and boilerplate clauses are exactly the kind of low-variance, high-volume drafting where a first AI draft can save real time — provided a lawyer still reviews every clause against the specific facts of the deal.
- **Summarising long, familiar-shaped documents.** A judgment, a lengthy regulatory circular, or a term sheet can be usefully summarised as a starting point for a human read — again, as a starting point, not a substitute for reading the primary source before advising on it.

## Where it still needs a very skeptical reader

- **Anything involving a specific citation.** Section numbers, case names, and regulatory citations are exactly the kind of specific, checkable fact that generative AI tools can get subtly wrong while sounding completely confident. Every citation an AI tool gives you needs to be checked against the primary source before it goes in front of a client or a court — the same standard this site's own Learn content holds itself to (see every topic's disclaimer and sources list).
- **Novel, fact-specific legal judgment.** Whether a particular clause is enforceable on *these* facts, in *this* jurisdiction, is still a lawyer's call — AI tools are trained on patterns in existing text, not on the specific facts of your matter.

## What this means for how you build skills

The practical shift isn't "learn to prompt an AI instead of learning the law" — it's the opposite. The lawyers getting real value from these tools are the ones who already know enough to **spot when the output is wrong**, quickly.
