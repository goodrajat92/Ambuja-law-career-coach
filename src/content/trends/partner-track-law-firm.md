The partner-track path is the one most corporate lawyers start on, whether or not they stay on it the whole way. What it actually rewards changes as you move up.

## Early years: technical depth and reliability

At the associate level, what gets you staffed on better deals is straightforward: **can you be handed a task and trusted to do it right without excessive supervision?** That means genuinely knowing the mechanics — not just having seen a term sheet before, but understanding *why* a clause is drafted the way it is (which is exactly the gap the Learn tab's topics are meant to close) — and being someone partners can hand a first draft to and get back something close to final.

## Mid-level: judgment and client-facing skill

Somewhere around mid-level, the bar shifts from "can you execute" to "can you exercise judgment and talk to clients directly." This is where the Practice tab's negotiation and client-communication exercises become directly relevant — a mid-level associate who can explain a tricky clause to a founder in plain language, or hold their own in a negotiation call, gets staffed very differently from one who can only draft.

## Senior levels: origination

The uncomfortable truth about partnership is that technical excellence alone rarely gets you there. At senior levels, **origination** — bringing in clients and work, not just executing it well — becomes the deciding factor at most firms. This is a genuinely different skill from legal craft, and it's worth starting to build relationships and a professional profile well before you're up for partnership consideration, not in the year you are.

## What to actually do with this

If partner track is the goal, the practical sequencing is: build technical depth early (don't skip it, even when AI tools make first drafts easier — see the AI trend brief), deliberately practise client-facing communication and negotiation once you have the technical base, and start thinking about origination — your professional network, your visibility, your own point of view on your practice area — years before it's formally evaluated.
