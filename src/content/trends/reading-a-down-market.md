Startup funding climates move in cycles: periods where capital is abundant and term sheets are founder-friendly, and periods where capital is scarcer and investors negotiate harder. The underlying law doesn't change between the two — the Companies Act, FEMA, and SEBI regulations that shape a term sheet are the same regardless of how "hot" the market is. What changes is **which terms actually get contested**.

## What tends to move in a cautious market

- **Liquidation preference structures.** Non-participating preferences are more likely to shift toward participating structures, or higher multiples, when investors are pricing in more downside risk. (See [Anti-Dilution Protection](/Ambuja-law-career-coach/learn/anti-dilution-protection) and the Negotiation exercise on liquidation preferences for the mechanics.)
- **Down-round anti-dilution protection becomes less theoretical.** In an exuberant market, anti-dilution clauses often go unused for years. In a cautious one, "what happens if we have to raise at a lower valuation" stops being a hypothetical founders wave away and becomes a real, near-term possibility worth negotiating harder over.
- **Board composition and investor consent rights.** Investors tend to ask for more board seats and a longer list of matters requiring investor consent when they're being more selective about where their capital goes.
- **Runway and use-of-funds scrutiny.** Diligence questions about burn rate and runway get more pointed, and conditions precedent tied to financial covenants show up more often.

## Why this matters for a deal lawyer specifically

Two practical consequences:

1. **The "standard" term sheet you learned on may not be standard right now.** What's market-standard is a moving target that tracks the funding climate, not a fixed legal rule — which is exactly why the Practice tab's negotiation exercises focus on *understanding the trade-off*, not memorising "the market number," which changes.
2. **Founders and first-time in-house counsel need this explained, not just drafted around.** A founder negotiating their first down round, or a company's first in-house counsel dealing with a tougher term sheet than the last round, benefits enormously from a lawyer who can explain *why* terms are tougher right now — not just redline the document. (See the Client Communication exercise on explaining anti-dilution for a worked example of that kind of explanation.)
