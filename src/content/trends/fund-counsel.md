Fund counsel — working as in-house legal for a VC or PE fund rather than advising them deal-by-deal from a firm — is a narrower but deeper path than general in-house work.

## What the job actually is

Where a firm associate might see a term sheet from one fund a handful of times a year, fund counsel sees their own fund's term sheet dozens of times — across every deal the fund does. That repetition builds a genuinely different kind of expertise: an instinctive sense of which terms are truly non-negotiable for the fund, which are relationship-dependent, and which vary by the specific portfolio company's situation. Nearly everything in this site's **VC & startup deals** Learn category and the term-sheet-related Practice exercises (CCPS, anti-dilution, liquidation preference negotiation, term sheet economics) is directly the daily material of this role.

## What's different from firm practice

- **One "client" whose preferences you know cold.** Unlike a firm lawyer who adapts to each new client's risk tolerance, fund counsel represents the same fund's interests deal after deal — which means you can (and are expected to) develop and enforce house positions on standard terms, rather than researching them fresh each time.
- **Portfolio-wide perspective.** Fund counsel often sees issues *across* portfolio companies before any single company's management does — a governance problem, an aggressive term becoming market-standard, a regulatory change affecting several holdings at once — and that pattern-recognition is part of the value fund counsel provides beyond just drafting.
- **Closer to the investment decision.** Depending on the fund and seniority, fund counsel can be involved earlier in diligence and deal evaluation than a purely execution-focused external counsel role would be, again rewarding the **business skills** side of legal training (reading financials, understanding valuation) alongside the legal drafting itself.

## How people usually get here

Common entry points are a firm associate role with heavy VC/PE deal exposure, or occasionally moving from in-house at a portfolio company into the fund itself once you understand the fund's perspective from the other side of the table. Either way, deep, repeated fluency in the standard VC term sheet — practised, not just read about — is the credential that actually matters most.
