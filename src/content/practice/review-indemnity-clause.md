  - "Flagged that the indemnity has no cap, and that this is a real (not just theoretical) risk for the indemnifying party."
  - "Flagged that there's no survival period / time limit for bringing a claim."
  - "Flagged the vague loss standard (\"any loss whatsoever\") and its breadth."
  - "Flagged the missing notice/procedure mechanism for claims (no requirement to notify promptly, no right to defend/settle)."
  - "Flagged the one-way (asymmetric) nature of the clause and asked whether that's actually the deal that was agreed commercially."
  - "Proposed at least one concrete redraft or added limitation for each issue flagged, not just a list of complaints."
modelAnswer:
  - "No cap: as drafted, the Seller's liability is unlimited and uncapped. Unless this is a deliberate, negotiated position (common for fundamental warranties like title, but unusual for the indemnity as a whole), this should be capped — commonly at the purchase price for fundamental matters and at a lower percentage of it for general/business warranties."
  - "No survival period: there is no time limit within which a claim must be brought, meaning the Seller is theoretically exposed indefinitely. A well-drafted clause fixes a survival period (e.g. 18-24 months for general warranties, longer — or the limitation period under law — for tax and title)."
  - "Vague loss standard: \"any loss whatsoever\" is broad enough to arguably include indirect, consequential, or even reputational loss, which sellers routinely resist. A tighter formulation ties the indemnity to direct losses \"arising out of or in connection with\" a defined breach, with indirect/consequential loss carved out (subject to negotiation)."
  - "No claims procedure: the clause is silent on what the Buyer must do before claiming — no notice requirement, no timeline, and critically no right for the Seller to participate in or control the defence/settlement of a third-party claim before paying out. This is a major gap: without it, the Buyer could settle a weak claim and pass the cost straight to the Seller with no opportunity to contest it."
  - "One-way structure: the clause as drafted only obligates the Seller to indemnify the Buyer. Depending on the deal, a Buyer-side indemnity (e.g. for the Buyer's post-completion conduct of the business, or for the Buyer's own breaches of the agreement) may be commercially expected and its absence worth raising with the client."
  - "A strong redraft adds: a cap (tied to purchase price, split by category of claim), a survival/time-bar clause, a defined \"Loss\" term excluding indirect/consequential loss (or a negotiated carve-back), and a notice-and-defence procedure requiring the Buyer to notify promptly and giving the Seller the right (at its cost) to control the defence of third-party claims."

## Brief

You act for the seller in a share purchase agreement. The buyer's draft includes this indemnity clause:

> *"The Seller shall indemnify and keep indemnified the Buyer against any loss whatsoever arising out of or in connection with any breach of the warranties set out in Schedule 3."*

## Instructions

This clause is a single sentence — and it's missing almost everything a properly negotiated indemnity needs. Read it as if it were handed to you the morning before signing, and write up the issues you'd raise with the buyer's counsel, in the order you'd raise them. For each issue, say **why** it matters commercially (not just "market practice says so") and propose a concrete fix or added limitation.
