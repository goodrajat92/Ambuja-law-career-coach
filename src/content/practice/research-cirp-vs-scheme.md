  - "Question presented: Given the company's cash-flow default to one large creditor but continued profitability, should the client pursue restructuring through a Section 12 CIRP under the IBC, or through a scheme of arrangement, and what does each route cost the company in time and control?"
  - "CIRP: triggered by a default (of the statutory minimum threshold) and filed at the NCLT; once admitted, a moratorium takes effect immediately and a resolution professional takes over management from the existing board (the promoters lose day-to-day control, a critical fact for this client). The process is bound by the Section 12 timeline (180 days, one 90-day extension, 330-day hard outer limit)."
  - "Scheme of arrangement: a court/NCLT-sanctioned restructuring under the Companies Act, generally initiated by the company itself (not necessarily triggered by default), requiring approval of the requisite majority of each class of creditors/members voting at a convened meeting, and then NCLT sanction. Crucially, existing management typically stays in control of the company throughout — no moratorium-triggered transfer of control to an insolvency professional in the way CIRP requires."
  - "Comparison for this client: since the company remains a going concern and profitable, and the underlying dispute is with one creditor rather than a general inability to pay across the board, a scheme of arrangement preserves management control and offers a negotiated route without the reputational and operational disruption of a CIRP filing (which, once admitted, is a public, creditor-driven process outside management's control). CIRP would only clearly be the better route if there are multiple defaulting creditors, a genuine going-concern risk, or if a scheme cannot realistically secure the requisite majority consent."
  - "Recommendation: pursue a negotiated resolution or scheme of arrangement in the first instance, given continued profitability and a single significant creditor dispute, while flagging to the client that if the creditor instead files a Section 7/9 application and it is admitted by the NCLT, control passes out of management's hands regardless of the company's preference — so parallel, proactive engagement with that creditor is time-sensitive."

## Brief

Your client is the promoter of a mid-sized manufacturing company. The company has defaulted on payment to one large operational creditor, but the business itself remains profitable and cash-generative overall. The client wants to know their options and, specifically, whether they will lose control of the company.

## Instructions

Write a short research memo (400-600 words) addressing:

1. The **question presented**, stated precisely.
2. The two realistic routes: a **CIRP filing under the IBC** and a **scheme of arrangement**, including who can trigger each and the effect on management control.
3. A **comparison** on timeline, consent thresholds, and control.
4. A **reasoned recommendation** for this client's specific facts, being explicit about what would change your answer.

Use the "Question presented → Short answer → Analysis → Recommendation" structure common to legal memos.
