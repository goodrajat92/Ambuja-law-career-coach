Before a single clause gets drafted, an M&A deal usually starts with one structural decision: are we buying the **company**, or are we buying the **business**?

## Share purchase: buy the company

In a share purchase, the buyer acquires shares in the target company from its existing shareholders. The company itself doesn't change — same legal entity, same PAN, same contracts, same employees, same history. What changes is who owns it.

The practical consequence: the buyer inherits **everything** the company already has — every contract (subject to any change-of-control consent needed under those contracts), every employee, every asset, and, critically, every **liability**, known or unknown, including contingent and historic ones. This is exactly why due diligence on a share purchase is comprehensive: the buyer is stepping into the target's shoes entirely.

## Business transfer: buy specific assets and liabilities

In a business transfer (also called an asset sale or, in its most common tax-defined form, a **slump sale**), the target company sells specific assets — and only the liabilities the parties agree to transfer — to the buyer, out of the existing company. The **selling company continues to exist**, now without the transferred business, and remains liable for whatever wasn't expressly transferred.

A **slump sale**, defined in **Section 2(42C) of the Income Tax Act, 1961**, is a particular species of business transfer: the transfer of one or more whole "undertakings" for a **lump-sum consideration**, without assigning individual values to the specific assets and liabilities being transferred (a valuation done purely for stamp duty purposes doesn't count as "assigning values" for this test). This lump-sum, no-itemisation feature is what triggers slump-sale tax treatment under **Section 50B** of the same Act, rather than item-by-item capital gains computation.

## Why the choice matters beyond the label

The two structures diverge on almost every practical axis:

- **Diligence scope** — a share purchase needs company-wide diligence (including hidden/contingent liabilities); a business transfer can, in principle, be scoped to the specific assets being acquired.
- **Consents** — a share purchase can trigger change-of-control consent clauses in the target's existing contracts even though the contracts themselves don't move; a business transfer requires actually assigning or novating each contract the buyer wants, one by one (unless a specific statutory mechanism, like a court/NCLT-approved scheme, is used instead).
- **Employees** — treatment differs significantly between the two structures, with specific statutory protections potentially applying to workers depending on how the transfer is structured — a fact-specific area worth its own dedicated diligence.
- **Stamp duty and tax** — the two routes attract different taxes and duties (capital gains on shares vs. business transfer tax treatment under Section 50B for a slump sale), which is frequently a major factor in the choice.

There's no universally "better" structure — the right choice depends on what liabilities the buyer is willing to inherit, what the seller is willing to warrant, and the tax and stamp duty position on each side.
