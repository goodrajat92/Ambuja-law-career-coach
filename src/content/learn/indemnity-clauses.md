Indemnity clauses show up in nearly every commercial contract a deal lawyer drafts or reviews — and the starting point for reading one, under Indian law, is two short sections of a 150-year-old statute.

## What the Act actually says

**Section 124** of the **Indian Contract Act, 1872** defines a contract of indemnity:

> A contract by which one party promises to save the other from loss caused to him by the conduct of the promisor himself, or by the conduct of any other person, is called a "contract of indemnity".

The party making the promise is the **indemnifier**; the party protected by it is the **indemnity-holder**.

**Section 125** then sets out what the indemnity-holder is entitled to recover, if sued in respect of a matter the indemnity covers — broadly, damages the indemnity-holder is compelled to pay, and costs reasonably incurred in defending or settling the suit, subject to the promise's terms.

Indian courts have historically read Section 124's definition somewhat narrowly compared to the English common-law concept of indemnity (which also covers loss from events outside anyone's "conduct," such as an accident or a natural event) — one of several reasons commercial drafting rarely relies on Sections 124–125 alone and instead spells out the indemnity's scope explicitly in the contract itself.

## What deal practice adds on top

An SHA or SPA indemnity clause is doing far more work than the bare Act requires, through negotiated drafting such as:

- **Specific indemnities** — for named, identified risks (a pending litigation, a known tax exposure) distinct from the general representations-and-warranties indemnity.
- **Caps** — an overall ceiling on indemnifiable losses, often carved out for fundamental warranties (like title and authority) which may be uncapped or capped at the full consideration.
- **Baskets/thresholds** — a minimum loss amount before any claim can be made at all (a "basket"), sometimes structured so that once the basket is crossed the whole amount is recoverable, not just the excess ("tipping basket") or only the excess ("deductible basket").
- **Survival periods** — a time window after closing within which a claim must be brought, often longer for fundamental or tax warranties than for general business warranties.

None of this is in the Contract Act — it is negotiated, deal-specific drafting, which is exactly why indemnity clauses in an SHA or SPA run to several pages rather than the two sections the Act itself devotes to the concept.

## Indemnity vs. representations and warranties

The two are closely related but not the same tool:

- A **representation or warranty** is a statement of fact (or a promise that a fact is true). Breach of a warranty is, in the first instance, a breach of contract, generally putting the injured party to proving loss flowing from the breach under ordinary contract-damages principles.
- An **indemnity** is a separate promise to make the other party whole for a defined loss, generally without the injured party needing to prove the loss was a foreseeable consequence of a breach in the way ordinary damages claims require.

That's why a well-drafted SHA/SPA typically has both: warranties to establish what was represented as true, and an indemnity to fix how losses arising from a breach (or from specifically identified risks) actually get compensated.
