Ask a first-time founder to describe their company's ownership and you'll usually get a percentage or two. Ask them to actually open the cap table, and the real structure — the one that determines outcomes in a negotiation or an exit — starts to show.

## What's actually in it

A capitalisation table lists, at minimum:

- every class of security issued (common/equity shares, each series of preference shares like CCPS, options, warrants, convertible notes),
- who holds each one,
- how many, and
- the price paid or the conversion terms attached.

## "Fully diluted" is the number that actually matters

Ownership can be described two ways: **as-issued** (what's actually outstanding today) and **fully diluted** (as-issued, *plus* everything that could become shares — the full ESOP pool whether granted or not, outstanding warrants, and any convertible instruments on an as-converted basis). Almost every negotiation — a new investor's percentage, a founder's real economic stake, a follow-on round's dilution — is discussed on a **fully diluted** basis, because that's the number that reflects what everyone will actually own once every outstanding right converts into shares.

## The ESOP pool trap

An unallocated ESOP pool sitting in the cap table looks like it belongs to "future employees," not to anyone in the room today. In practice, it dilutes **existing shareholders** the moment it's created — whether or not a single option is ever granted. This is exactly the mechanic behind the common negotiating point covered in [Break Down a Term Sheet's Economic Terms](/Ambuja-law-career-coach/practice/analysis/analyze-term-sheet-terms): a pre-money ESOP top-up is paid for entirely by the founders and any earlier investors, not by the new money coming in.

## Preference shares show up twice

Because instruments like CCPS (see [CCPS Explained](/Ambuja-law-career-coach/learn/ccps-explained)) carry different rights before and after conversion, a well-built cap table typically shows them **both ways**: as-issued (the actual preference shares outstanding, with their liquidation preference and voting terms) and as-converted (their fully diluted equity-equivalent share count). Reading only one view can genuinely mislead — an investor's as-issued shareholding might look modest while their as-converted, fully diluted percentage (the number that matters for voting and for most economic calculations) is considerably larger.

## Why this matters in an exit

The cap table on its own tells you percentages. Read alongside the **liquidation preference stack** (which investor gets paid first, and how much, before anyone else sees a rupee — see [Anti-Dilution Protection](/Ambuja-law-career-coach/learn/anti-dilution-protection) for how conversion ratios interact with this), it tells you the question that actually matters in anything short of a spectacular exit: **who gets paid, in what order, and how much is left for the founders and common shareholders after the preference stack is satisfied.** That's the calculation a competent deal lawyer should be able to run for a client before a term sheet is signed — not just after an exit happens.
