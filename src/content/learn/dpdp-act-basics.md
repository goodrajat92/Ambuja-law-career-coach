Every commercial contract that touches customer or employee data now sits under a statute most lawyers are still getting used to: the **Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDP Act)** — India's first comprehensive personal data protection law.

## The two roles the Act is built around

- **Data Fiduciary** — whoever, alone or with others, determines the **purpose and means** of processing personal data. In a commercial contract, this is usually the company collecting the data (a platform, an employer, a service provider) — though a contract can also make one party a fiduciary and another a processor acting on its instructions, a distinction worth getting right in the drafting.
- **Data Principal** — the individual the personal data is *about*. Their rights are the point of the whole Act.

## Consent is the operative concept

Processing personal data needs the Data Principal's **consent**, and the Act is specific about what that means: consent must be **free, specific, informed, unconditional and unambiguous**, given through a **clear affirmative action** — not inferred from silence, a pre-ticked box, or bundled into unrelated terms. Before asking for consent, the Data Fiduciary must give a **notice** describing what data is collected and why.

Two features matter a lot in practice:

1. **Consent must be as easy to withdraw as it was to give.** A Data Principal can withdraw consent at any time, and the Act requires the withdrawal process to be comparably easy — a company that makes signing up trivial and canceling deliberately hard is exactly the pattern this is meant to prevent.
2. **Security is a standalone obligation.** A Data Fiduciary must take **reasonable security safeguards** to prevent a personal data breach — this exists independent of whether consent was properly obtained in the first place.

## Children's data gets its own, stricter rule

Where the Data Principal is a child, **consent must come from a parent**, and — separately from the consent question — a Data Fiduciary is prohibited outright from **behavioural tracking or targeted advertising** directed at children. This is a flat prohibition, not something a parent's consent can override.

## Why this belongs in a deal lawyer's toolkit

Data protection compliance now shows up directly in deal practice: **diligence** on a target's data-handling practices and consent records, **indemnity and warranty drafting** around data protection compliance in an SPA (see [Indemnity Clauses](/Ambuja-law-career-coach/learn/indemnity-clauses)), and **vendor/data-processing agreements** that need to correctly allocate Data Fiduciary vs. processor obligations between the parties.
