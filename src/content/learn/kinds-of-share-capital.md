Every cap table, however elaborate, is built on a distinction the Companies Act draws in one short section: **Section 43** splits a company's share capital into just two kinds — **equity share capital** and **preference share capital**. Understanding this split is the foundation for reading a term sheet, a shareholders' agreement, or a set of articles.

## Equity share capital

The Act defines equity share capital almost by exclusion: it is **all share capital which is not preference share capital**. Equity shares may:

- carry ordinary voting rights (one vote per share, the default), or
- carry **differential rights** as to dividend, voting, or otherwise, if such rights are permitted under the rules made under the Act.

This is the hook that lets companies issue, for example, non-voting equity or equity with disproportionate voting rights — subject to conditions in subordinate rules, not the Act itself.

## Preference share capital

Preference share capital is defined by what it is *preferred* over ordinary equity, on two specific points:

1. **Dividend** — a preferential right to a fixed amount, or an amount calculated at a fixed rate.
2. **Repayment of capital** — a preferential right to repayment of capital (or a fixed premium) in a winding up, ahead of equity.

Preference shares can *also* carry a right to participate in further dividends or surplus capital beyond the fixed preferential amount, and they don't lose their character as preference shares merely because of that additional right.

## Why this matters for deal lawyers

Almost every instrument a VC or PE investor actually holds — **CCPS** (compulsorily convertible preference shares), for instance — is a preference share under this Section 43 definition, with a conversion mechanism layered on top by contract (the shareholders' agreement) and by the share terms themselves. See [CCPS explained](/Ambuja-law-career-coach/learn/ccps-explained) for how that layering works in practice.
