An investor negotiating a term sheet is really negotiating a price today for a share of tomorrow's company. Anti-dilution protection is the clause that decides what happens to that price if tomorrow turns out worse than expected.

## The problem it solves

Say an investor buys CCPS today priced to convert 1:1 into equity, based on a valuation of ₹100 per share. A year later, the company runs low on cash and has to raise a **down round** — new shares priced at ₹60. Without any protection, the earlier investor's stake is now priced above the market: their shares are worth what the market says they're worth, not what they paid. Anti-dilution protection is the earlier investor's insurance against exactly this.

Mechanically, it works by **adjusting the conversion ratio** of the investor's CCPS (see [CCPS Explained](/Ambuja-law-career-coach/learn/ccps-explained)) — so instead of converting 1:1, the investor's preference shares convert into *more* equity shares, softening the effective loss.

## The two standard formulas

**Full ratchet.** The earlier investor's conversion price is reset to match the new, lower round price — full stop, regardless of how many shares were issued in the down round. This is the most investor-friendly version and correspondingly rare in negotiated deals, because of how harshly it dilutes the founders and other shareholders.

**Weighted average.** The conversion price is adjusted by a formula that weighs the size of the down round against the company's total existing capitalisation — a large down round moves the price more than a small one. This is the market standard in the overwhelming majority of Indian (and global) VC deals, because it spreads the dilution more proportionately.

Weighted average protection is itself commonly split into two variants:

- **Broad-based**, where the calculation's "total shares outstanding" figure includes a wide base — typically all common/equity shares, all preference shares on an as-converted basis, and shares reserved under an ESOP pool.
- **Narrow-based**, where that base is defined more narrowly (often just common/equity shares), which produces a larger price adjustment — and is correspondingly more investor-friendly and less common.

**Broad-based weighted average** is the version you'll meet most often in Indian term sheets.

## What actually gets negotiated

Because "anti-dilution protection: yes/no" is rarely the real fight, the negotiation usually turns on:

- **which formula** (weighted average, almost always, in practice),
- **broad-based vs narrow-based** (broad-based, in most negotiated outcomes),
- **carve-outs** — down rounds that don't trigger the adjustment at all, commonly including ESOP issuances, shares issued on conversion of existing convertible instruments, and bonus issues.
