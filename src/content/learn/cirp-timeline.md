The **Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016** was built around one central idea: insolvency resolution should be **time-bound**, not an open-ended process that drags on for years the way winding-up petitions historically did. Section 12 is where that idea is written into law.

## The three numbers to know

**180 days — the standard period.** Section 12(1) provides that the Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process ("**CIRP**") shall ordinarily be completed within **180 days** from the date the Adjudicating Authority (the National Company Law Tribunal, or NCLT) admits the insolvency application.

**90 days — the one allowed extension.** Section 12(2) allows a **single extension**, of up to 90 days, on an application to the Adjudicating Authority — but only if the Committee of Creditors first passes a resolution approving the extension by a vote of **66% of the voting share**.

**330 days — the hard outer limit.** Section 12(3) then caps the *entire* process — the initial 180 days, plus any 90-day extension, plus any time spent in legal proceedings connected with the CIRP — at a mandatory **330 days**. If CIRP isn't completed within that window, the corporate debtor ordinarily moves toward liquidation.

## Why the 330-day cap matters (and why it's litigated)

The 330-day limit was added specifically to stop the extension mechanism, and satellite litigation, from quietly turning a "180-day" process into a multi-year one — which is exactly what had started happening in practice. But because the cap explicitly includes "time taken in legal proceedings," and because resolution processes for complex, asset-heavy companies routinely attract litigation (from dissatisfied bidders, promoters, or creditors), the interaction between the mandatory 330-day cap and a resolution professional's ability to actually complete a fair process has itself been a recurring subject of litigation and judicial interpretation.

## What this means in practice

For a lawyer working on an IBC matter, the Section 12 timeline is the frame everything else sits inside: the resolution professional's process for inviting and evaluating resolution plans, the Committee of Creditors' deliberations, and any related litigation are all racing against these three numbers. Missing them isn't just a procedural embarrassment — it can determine whether the company is rescued through a resolution plan, or ends up in liquidation instead.
